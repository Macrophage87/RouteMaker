Routing

RouteMaker turns the stress ratings into a route by giving every stretch of road, every junction and every closure a cost, then finding the route that costs least for the ride type you chose. This page lists those costs and how each ride type changes them. How a road gets its level is on Stress ratings.

The routing engine

The routing engine is Valhalla[1], an open source router. RouteMaker builds its own copy of Valhalla's road graph each week with the stress levels written in, then tunes Valhalla's bike costing for each ride type. On top of that sits RouteMaker's own search, which asks Valhalla for alternatives, excludes busy stretches and bad crossings, and asks again, up to five rounds.

What each level costs

A route is chosen by adding up the cost of every stretch it rides, in calm miles. A mile of quiet street counts as 1. A busier stretch costs more, as if it were longer. On the Default ride type a mile of LTS 3 costs about 2.9 to 5.8 calm miles and a mile of LTS 4 about 13 to 16; faster and wider roads of the same level cost more.

The traffic slider sets how much more. The ride type sets where the slider starts:

Everything a route pays for

  • Each stretch, by its stress level: the calm-mile costs above. Faster and wider roads of the same level cost more. A half step costs halfway between its two levels.
  • Each junction, by what you cross or turn across and what controls it: see Junctions.
  • Each entry onto an Avoid road: about 2.5 calm miles [4 km] more.
  • Riding the same road twice: a repeated stretch counts 1.5 times, but never at the price of more busy road.
  • Hills, by the Hills slider, and surface, by the ride type: rough DC pavement[3] costs a little more, and each ride type has its own view of gravel and trails.
  • Turns: each change of street costs time, 5 seconds on Default and 30 on Group Ride.
  • Closures: see Closures.
  • Exposure to busy roads, above 80 on the slider: a second search weighs each route's riding on LTS 3, LTS 4 and Avoid, and accepts a longer route for less of it, up to a limit. Ride types shows how heavily each ride type counts it.

None of these change a road's rating: they change only what a route through it costs.

Junctions

Crossing or turning onto a busy road is often the most stressful part of a ride, so junctions have a cost of their own, measured in calm miles: the extra distance of quiet street a junction is worth. Quiet streets meeting quiet streets cost almost nothing.

Junctions costing at least 800 ft [244 m] are marked orange in the route description, and those costing at least 3,100 ft [945 m] are marked red. Each warning describes the road, the movement and the control in words.

The control at a junction (a signal, a stop sign or none) comes from OpenStreetMap[10]. From the version 0.10.0 map, the traffic signal lists of the District, Maryland's State Highway Administration, Virginia's DOT and Alexandria fill the gaps: a junction where the agency lists a signal and OpenStreetMap maps none is treated as signalled. Virginia's list is a snapshot from October 2023, so a signal built since then is not in it.

How a ride type changes the costs

Choosing a ride type changes these numbers, not the ratings. It sets where the traffic slider starts, how much each level costs per mile, how fast you are assumed to ride, how hills and surfaces count, and for some types which ways are left out[2]. Novice and Nervous reads every road and junction one level busier. Fast, at the other end of the slider, prices a mile of LTS 4 at only about 2.1 to 2.3 calm miles against Default's 13 to 16, so where a shorter way runs on busy roads, Fast takes it and Default does not. Ride types has every ride type, and three of them work quite differently: Mass Ride and Mountain Bike, Mountain Goat and Gravel.

What the comparison shows:

  • Default (slider 70) asks the routing engine once, with its own costs per level. It already keeps off busy roads: no LTS 4 at all, and most of the way on cycle tracks.
  • Trailmaxxing (slider 100) then runs the calm search: it asked for alternatives and left out the busiest stretches for two rounds, and took a different way down 17th Street, L Street and 11th Street. Its LTS 1 share rose from 73% to 85%. It rides slightly more LTS 3 (0.31 mi [0.50 km] against 0.26 mi [0.42 km]), but much less LTS 2, so its stress exposure, the busy riding weighted by level, fell from 2,730 ft to 2,150 ft [832 m to 654 m].
  • Here the calmer route is also shorter. That is not the rule: the calm search accepts a longer route when it buys enough calm, up to a limit, and keeps Default's route when it finds nothing better. In two other trips tried the same day, the two ride types gave the same route.

Closures

Only Maryland and Virginia publish work-zone feeds in the national Work Zone Data Exchange format[11]: dated, mapped closures in force, with whether bikes can pass. The District publishes no such feed. What it has instead is a mix: police traffic advisories and AlertDC emergency closures[12], which name streets in words and are matched to the map; DDOT's TOPS permits[13], which say a street may be occupied some time in the coming month, not that it is closed now; and National Park Service alerts for the parks. So a closure in the District is usually known from an advisory or a reviewer, not from an official work-zone record, and planned roadwork in the District can be missing until someone reports it. Virginia's feed also needs an access key, so it is read only where one is set up.

  • State work zones from Maryland's and Virginia's feeds[11]: a road closed to bikes is left out; a closure that does not mention bikes costs about 10 minutes and gets a warning; lane and shoulder work cost a little.
  • Police and city alerts (MPD traffic advisories and AlertDC)[12] are matched to the streets they name and routed around.
  • Reviewed closures, entered or confirmed by a RouteMaker reviewer, are routed around and credited to the reviewer. Saved routes get an alert when one affects them.
  • Road closed in a ride marks the road ahead closed for that ride only. Where a route detours round a closure, the standard way is shown too, and you are followed on whichever you take.
  • DDOT TOPS permits[13] are permission to occupy a street some time in the coming month. They never move a route, in Go, Plan or anywhere else, and they raise no warnings. Plan has a DC permits layer, off by default, for anyone who wants to look.
  • National Park Service alerts[12] are park-wide notices, shown as a note on a route through the park, never as closures.

While you ride

  • Cues. The first cue comes about 27 seconds before a turn, the second about 10 seconds before, and "now" at the turn. Cues say only the turn and the street. You choose screen reader, RouteMaker's voice, both or neither.
  • Off route. You are off route when you are more than 100 ft [30 m] from the line (more when GPS is poor, up to 330 ft [100 m]) for three readings over 8 seconds. Readings less accurate than 160 ft [50 m] are not used.
  • Re-routing plans on from where you are, in the direction you are heading.
  • Stops. Tapping a food, sight, repair or water stop, or a lettered stop, opens a card with what is there, whether it is open now (where its mapped hours can be read), how far it is or the detour, and a Website link when one is mapped. A tap never changes the route: only Route here or Add as stop does. Stops come from OpenStreetMap, so check a repair stand works before you count on it.
