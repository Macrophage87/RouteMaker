Routing

RouteMaker turns the stress ratings into a route by giving every stretch of road, every junction and every closure a cost, then finding the route that costs least for the ride type you chose. This page lists those costs and how each ride type changes them. How a road gets its level is on Stress ratings.

At a glance

  • Every stretch costs its length times its level's cost, in calm miles: a mile of quiet street counts as 1.
  • Busy junctions cost extra, so a quiet street that crosses a highway at a stop sign is not treated as quiet.
  • Your ride type and the traffic slider set the costs; they never change a road's rating.
  • Real closures move a route. DC's planned-work permits never do.

Looking for something else? How a road gets its level: Stress ratings. Each ride type's settings: Ride types.

The routing engine

The routing engine is Valhalla[1], an open source router. RouteMaker builds its own copy of Valhalla's road graph each week with the stress levels written in, then tunes Valhalla's bike costing for each ride type. On top of that sits RouteMaker's own search, which asks Valhalla for alternatives, excludes busy stretches and bad crossings, and asks again, up to five rounds.

What each level costs

A route is chosen by adding up the cost of every stretch it rides, in calm miles. A mile of quiet street counts as 1. A busier stretch costs more, as if it were longer. On the Default ride type a mile of LTS 3 costs about 2.9 to 5.8 calm miles and a mile of LTS 4 about 13 to 16; faster and wider roads of the same level cost more.

Everything a route pays for

A route pays for every stretch by its stress level, every junction it crosses, each entry onto an Avoid road, cobblestones and rails (from the next map update), repeated roads, hills, surface and turns. All of it is counted in calm miles.

More: Everything a route pays for
  • Each stretch, by its stress level: the calm-mile costs above. Faster and wider roads of the same level cost more. A half step costs halfway between its two levels.
  • Each junction, by what you cross or turn across and what controls it: see Junctions.
  • Each entry onto an Avoid road: about 2.5 calm miles [4 km] more.
  • Cobblestones and rails in the road, from the next map update, such as Georgetown's O and P Streets NW or the streetcar tracks on H Street NE: priced as heavily as a very busy road, so a route uses one only when the way round is as bad. Not on Mass Ride, and not on paths. See Cobblestones and rails.
  • Riding the same road twice: a repeated stretch counts 1.5 times, but never at the price of more busy road.
  • Hills, by the Hills slider (see Hills), and surface, by the ride type: rough DC pavement[3] costs a little more, and each ride type has its own view of gravel and trails.
  • Turns: each change of street costs time, 5 seconds on Default and 30 on Group Ride.
  • Stops and signals: the time a rider loses at each junction they give way at or wait at, counted as that much quiet riding: see Time lost at junctions.
  • Closures: see Closures.
  • Exposure to busy roads, above 80 on the slider: a second search weighs each route's riding on LTS 3, LTS 4 and Avoid, and accepts a longer route for less of it, up to a limit. Ride types shows how heavily each ride type counts it.

None of these change a road's rating: they change only what a route through it costs.

Junctions

Crossing or turning onto a busy road is often the most stressful part of a ride, so junctions have a cost of their own, measured in calm miles: the extra distance of quiet street a junction is worth. Quiet streets meeting quiet streets cost almost nothing.

More: Junctions

Junctions costing at least 800 ft [244 m] are marked orange in the route description, and those costing at least 3,100 ft [945 m] are marked red. Each warning describes the road, the movement and the control in words.

The control at a junction (a signal, a stop sign or none) comes from OpenStreetMap[10]. From the next map update, the traffic signal lists of the District, Maryland's State Highway Administration and Alexandria fill the gaps: a junction where the agency lists a signal and OpenStreetMap maps none is treated as signalled. Virginia's DOT list, a snapshot from October 2023, is used only for comparison and never marks a junction.

Some junctions OpenStreetMap maps with a signal really have a flashing light. Where Maryland's State Highway Administration lists one as a warning beacon or an intersection control beacon (it lists about 900 beacons), or RouteMaker's owner has marked it, the junction is not treated as signalled, and the route description says "flashing light, not a signal". Between roads of similar stress the light is taken as flashing red both ways, an all-way stop. Where a quieter road crosses a busier one, the busier road keeps going, and crossing it is priced as stopping and waiting for a gap (see Time lost at junctions).

Time lost at junctions

The routing engine times a junction at about a second, whatever controls it, so a route through a grid of stop signs used to be timed as quick as a trail of the same length. From version 0.10.0, route times and the choice between routes also count the time a rider loses at each junction on the route:

Time lost at a junction

Junctions a few yards apart, such as the two halves of a divided road, count once, at the dearest. A flashing light is priced by who gives way, not as a signal, so crossing a busier road at one costs the wait for a gap, 12, 20 or 30 seconds by how busy that road is, rather than the 15 seconds of a signal. This is time, not stress: a junction's cost for traffic is still the one above. The same time is shown in the route's time and counted in the route search as that much quiet riding, so of two equally calm routes about the same length, the one with fewer stops is preferred.

Hills

Hills count in the same calm miles as traffic. The Hills slider is a grade: the steepest you can climb for several minutes in your lowest gears without labored breathing, from 1% to 15%, with 8% in the middle. Every climb costs the time it takes. Past your grade a climb also costs a penalty: grades past yours count for more the harder and longer they get, and a short steep pitch you can power up counts for little. Having to push the bike is a last resort. The penalty only helps choose a route: no time you see includes it. Avoiding hills never makes a route busier. Not sure of your grade? Leave the slider where your ride type starts it: a rough guess plans much the same routes.

More: Hills

Time, always

RouteMaker works a climb out with the standard cycling power model[14][15]. Grades are read over at least 1,000 ft [300 m], because the elevation data[16], sampled every 98 ft [30 m], is too noisy for steeper pitches over a shorter distance to be trusted, and a short kick averages out. On each rise a standard rider and bike of 198 lb [90 kg] climb at an easy endurance pace, 65% of FTP, never faster than the ride's speed on the flat and never slower than 5 mph [8 km/h]. The extra seconds over the flat cost what the same seconds of flat riding do. Your weight in Settings moves at that same power, so a loaded bike climbs slower, down to walking pace; the penalty below does not change with weight, since loaded bikes usually have low gears.

Past your grade

The penalty follows a power curve, though you set only the grade: your grade is where holding 5 mph [8 km/h] in a low gear takes the top of Zone 2, 75% of FTP[17], about where breathing starts to get harder[18]. Where a climb takes more than the endurance pace, each second of it adds a penalty that grows quickly with the power past that pace:

How long a climb lasts. You can hold more power for two minutes than for twenty, so each stretch is weighed as the steady effort it equals over the whole climb, by the critical power model[19]: the power it takes divided by 1 plus (70 seconds ÷ the climb's riding time). A 2-minute pitch weighs a little over a third less, a 20-minute climb about 5% less, and your own grade held for several minutes costs nothing past its time. A climb runs until the road drops 10 ft [3 m] below its top or levels off for about 1,500 ft [450 m], so small bumps in the elevation data and the points you add do not split it into short pitches. At 3%, a 1,000 ft [300 m] pitch at 6% costs 1.2 minutes of extra time and 1.0 minute of penalty.

Pushing. Where riding a stretch would cost more than pushing the bike up it, it is pushed, at three quarters of the standard hiking speed[20]: 1.9 mph [3.1 km/h] at 6%, 1.7 mph [2.7 km/h] at 10% and 1.4 mph [2.2 km/h] at 15%. Each second pushed costs its time plus 4 seconds of penalty, so a route you would have to push loses to almost any you can ride.

Choosing among routes

Away from the middle of the slider, RouteMaker asks the routing engine[1] for a few routes, for a start and an end up to 16 mi [25 km] apart. Left of the middle the engine's own hill penalty, full at 1%, shapes which ones it offers. RouteMaker then takes the engine's climbing charge off each route, puts your hill cost in its place, and keeps the best; left of the middle, descents steep enough to ride the brakes count there too, more toward 1%. In the calm search, at any setting, the distance a route is charged for counts its climbing as the flat riding it is worth.

Hills and traffic together

In the calm search, a busy stretch counts more when it climbs: each stretch's busy riding is multiplied by a hill factor of 1 plus a share of the stretch's extra effort. The share is 0.25 at the middle of the slider, 0.5 at the far left and none at the far right. Climbs count from 3%, and descents past the ride type's braking grade count too. A 0.62 mi [1 km] climb at 4% on an LTS 3 road has a factor of about 1.7, so it counts as about 0.43 mi [0.69 km] more LTS 3 riding than the same road on the flat; at 6%, about 2.0 and 0.64 mi [1.03 km]. On LTS 4, which weighs twice as much, it counts twice that. The map's colors and the ratings do not change.

Mountain Goat

Mountain Goat's slider keeps its own scale: right of the middle it seeks hills. The route that climbs most wins, within a distance budget of up to 1.25 times the length of the router's direct route halfway right and 1.5 times at the far right. At the top of the Traffic slider the calm search decides instead, and among equally calm routes the one that climbs more ranks first. See Mountain Bike, Mountain Goat and Gravel.

How a ride type changes the costs

Choosing a ride type changes these numbers, not the ratings. It sets where the traffic slider starts, how much each level costs per mile, how fast you are assumed to ride, how hills and surfaces count, and for some types which ways are left out[2]. Novice and Nervous reads every road and junction one level busier. Fast, at the other end of the slider, prices a mile of LTS 4 at only about 2.1 to 2.3 calm miles against Default's 13 to 16, so where a shorter way runs on busy roads, Fast takes it and Default does not. Ride types has every ride type, and three of them work quite differently: Mass Ride and Mountain Bike, Mountain Goat and Gravel.

A worked example

A short busy block can outweigh a shorter route. From Dupont Circle to Nationals Park, a route of 4.4 mi [7.0 km] starts with one LTS 4 block, so both Default and Trailmaxxing take a calmer route of 4.7 mi [7.5 km] instead.

  • The block. The shorter route starts down 18th Street NW from Massachusetts Avenue to N Street: about 400 ft [120 m] of three-lane road with no bike lane, which DDOT rates LTS 4[21]. RouteMaker keeps the agency's rating, since an agency's data outranks the formula and a rating is lowered only with a clear reason.
  • What it costs. In the routing engine's own cost, that one block counts for about 1,000 seconds of riding, under Default's costs and Trailmaxxing's alike. That is far more than the shorter route saves, so the longer route wins. Nothing else on the shorter route, such as its gates, bollards or short path links, costs much.
  • Not calmer elsewhere either. Past that block, the shorter route has more LTS 3 than the longer one: 7.1% of the way against 5.5%.
  • The lesson. The cheapest route is not always the shortest: one short stretch on a busy road can be worth a detour of a third of a mile [half a kilometre] or more.

Closures

Only Maryland and Virginia publish work-zone feeds in the national Work Zone Data Exchange format[22]: dated, mapped closures in force, with whether bikes can pass. The District publishes no such feed. What it has instead is a mix: police traffic advisories and AlertDC emergency closures[23], which name streets in words and are matched to the map; DDOT's TOPS permits[24], which say a street may be occupied some time in the coming month, not that it is closed now; and National Park Service alerts for the parks. So a closure in the District is usually known from an advisory or a reviewer, not from an official work-zone record, and planned roadwork in the District can be missing until someone reports it. Virginia's feed also needs an access key, so it is read only where one is set up.

More: Closures
  • State work zones from Maryland's and Virginia's feeds[22]: a road closed to bikes is left out; a closure that does not mention bikes costs about 10 minutes and gets a warning; lane and shoulder work cost a little.
  • Police and city alerts (MPD traffic advisories and AlertDC)[23] are matched to the streets they name and routed around.
  • Reviewed closures, entered or confirmed by a RouteMaker reviewer, are routed around and credited to the reviewer. Saved routes get an alert when one affects them.
  • Road closed in a ride marks the road ahead closed for that ride only. Where a route detours round a closure, the standard way is shown too, and you are followed on whichever you take. On ride types that skip dirt trails, Road closed on a dirt trail is taken as a sign the trails are in bad shape: the rest of that ride's re-routes stay off dirt trails, even to a stop on one, and a new plan opts back in. A road that only crosses a dirt trail does not count.
  • DDOT TOPS permits[24] are permission to occupy a street some time in the coming month. They never move a route, in Go, Plan or anywhere else, and they raise no warnings. Plan has a DC permits layer, off by default, for anyone who wants to look.
  • National Park Service alerts[23] are park-wide notices, shown as a note on a route through the park, never as closures.

While you ride

More: While you ride
  • Cues. The first cue comes about 27 seconds before a turn, the second about 10 seconds before, and "now" at the turn. Cues say only the turn and the street. You choose screen reader, RouteMaker's voice, both or neither.
  • Off route. You are off route when you are more than 100 ft [30 m] from the line (more when GPS is poor, up to 330 ft [100 m]) for three readings over 8 seconds. Readings less accurate than 160 ft [50 m] are not used.
  • Re-routing plans on from where you are, in the direction you are heading.
  • A U-turn you keep riding past. After two "Make a U-turn" re-routes in a row that you ride on past, the third keeps your way: "New route found. Keep going ahead." If there is no way ahead, it says "No route found ahead. Make a U-turn." once and leaves that route up, then checks quietly about once a minute for a way ahead and speaks only if it finds one. Turning round as asked, or a re-route without a U-turn, starts the count again.
  • Stops. Tapping a food, sight, repair or water stop, or a lettered stop, opens a card with what is there, whether it is open now (where its mapped hours can be read), how far it is or the detour, and a Website link when one is mapped. A tap never changes the route: only Route here or Add as stop does. Stops come from OpenStreetMap, so check a repair stand works before you count on it.
  • End of a ride. When you end a ride you see its summary: distance, moving time, average speed, total time, climbing, an elevation chart, and the share of the ride on each traffic stress level. Done clears Go for your next trip; in Plan the plan stays so you can adjust it. Undo picks the ride back up, keeping the figures recorded so far. Nothing about the ride is saved or sent.
  • Where the summary's figures come from. Distance and times come from the ride's own GPS readings; wander under 25 ft [8 m] and impossible jumps are ignored. Climbing, elevation and stress come from the planned route, along the parts you actually rode on it, so a detour adds distance but no climbing.
