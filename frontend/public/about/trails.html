Mountain Bike, Mountain Goat and Gravel

Three of RouteMaker's ride types leave the pavement: Gravel, Mountain Goat and Mountain Bike. Each plans differently from the everyday ride types. This page covers the trails they use, how trails are rated, the Climbs and topo layers, and what a tap on a trail tells you. For every other ride type, see Adventure ride types.

At a glance

  • Mountain Bike is for riding mountain-bike trails. It looks for them, up to the hardest level you choose, and links them with calm roads.
  • Mountain Goat is for climbing. It looks for categorized climbs near your line.
  • Gravel is for mixed surfaces. Unpaved roads and paths cost nothing extra, so they compete with pavement.
  • Gravel and Mountain Goat may also ride unrated trails and the easiest rated ones (Green). Other ride types never plan onto a trail, and most route to a point you put on an unrated dirt trail.
  • Tap a trail for its surface, an elevation profile and who can ride it. Tap a climb's marker for its profile.

Looking for something else? Every other ride type: Ride types. How roads between trails are rated: Stress ratings.

Mountain Bike: for trails

Mountain Bike's job is to put you on mountain-bike trails. It is the only ride type that goes looking for them, and it plans on its own map, where mountain-bike trails are open.

More: Mountain Bike: for trails
  • Hardest trail level: 1 to 4, default 2 (Blue). Routes skip trails above it. If a route cannot avoid one, RouteMaker still shows it and warns you.
  • Preferred trail level: 1 to 4, default 2, never above the hardest level. Trails near it are favored.
  • Trail seeking: a mile of trail counts as half a mile of road when routes are compared. A mile at your preferred level counts as 0.4, and one level away as 0.45.
  • Connecting roads: LTS 1 and 2 freely. LTS 3 only for gaps under 0.5 mi [0.8 km]. LTS 4 and Avoid only where no route without them exists. See stress ratings in detail.
  • Loop: plans a loop from your start, unless you turn the loop off.
  • Unrated trails count as level 2.
  • Wet trails: a trail mapped as closed when wet[5] is never used. RouteMaker cannot know today's ground, so the trail panel links to MORE's trail conditions[6].
  • One-way trails are ridden one way only.

In Mountain Bike, trails up to your hardest level are drawn as lines to ride. Harder ones keep their level's look.

Mountain Goat: for climbs

Mountain Goat's job is to find climbs. It starts the hills slider at the top, where it seeks climbs. It asks the router for routes, adds routes through categorized climbs near your line, and picks the one that climbs most. A route may be up to half again as long as the most direct one (1.5 times). Lower on the slider the allowance shrinks: at 50 it is 1.25 times.

More: Mountain Goat: for climbs

It does not add climb routes to a loop, which would only go up a climb and back down. Rough surfaces cost very little. It may ride unrated trails and Green trails, as Gravel does.

Gravel: for mixed surfaces

Gravel's job is a ride that mixes pavement with gravel and dirt. It plans as a cyclocross bike. Unpaved roads and paths cost nothing extra, so a gravel road competes with pavement on distance and traffic alone. It does not yet look for unpaved roads on purpose. It may ride unrated trails and Green trails. The traffic slider starts at 40, so it accepts busier roads than Default.

Trail ratings and who may ride them

More: Trail ratings and who may ride them

Where the ratings come from

RouteMaker does not rate trails itself. It reads the ratings mappers give trails in OpenStreetMap. Two tags rate how hard a trail is to ride:

  • mtb:scale, from S0 (easy) to S6 (hardest), describes the trail's technical difficulty[1].
  • mtb:scale:imba, from 0 to 4, follows the International Mountain Bicycling Association's trail difficulty ratings, the green, blue and black symbols seen at trailheads[2].

Why there are two scales, and why they are merged

The two scales were made for different jobs:

  • mtb:scale comes from the Singletrail-Skala, a European scale for alpine singletrack[1]. It rates only how technical the riding surface is in dry weather: S0 is a smooth gravel or dirt path, S1 has roots and loose stones, S2 bigger roots, steps and tight turns, and S3 to S6 are blocky, steep or exposed ground that few riders can ride. It says nothing about a trail's length, climbing or how easy it is to get lost.
  • The IMBA rating is the American trailhead system, borrowed from ski slopes: a green circle (easy), a blue square (more difficult), a black diamond (very difficult) and a double black diamond (extremely difficult)[2]. It rates the whole trail as a ride, taking in its width, grade, surface and obstacles. It is what the signs at local trail systems show.

Mappers around here use one tag, the other, or both, and they do not always agree. A route needs one answer to "is this trail within the hardest level I chose?", and the map needs one color per trail. So RouteMaker reads both onto one four-step scale, and when they disagree it takes the harder one, so a trail is never shown easier than a mapper rated it.

The two scales overlap step for step at the easy end. The project compared them and set S0 equal to IMBA 0, S1 to IMBA 1, and so on up to S4 and IMBA 4. mtb:scale has two harder steps, S5 and S6, which IMBA does not, so S4 to S6 all become level 4.

  • One tag only: its level is used.
  • Both tags, and they disagree: the harder one wins, so a trail is never shown easier than a mapper rated it.
  • A range or a plus sign: "1-2" counts as 2, "2+" as 2. A number past the top of a scale counts as level 4.
  • A rating of 0: the trail is an easy gravel or dirt trail, not a mountain-bike level. If the other tag says 1 or more, that level is used.
  • Neither tag: the trail is Not rated. It is drawn in gray dashes, and its panel says "Not rated: no difficulty is mapped for this trail."
  • A paved trail is never treated as singletrack, even if it carries a rating.

Other tags do not change the level. The hiking scale (sac_scale) and how easy a trail is to follow (trail_visibility) help decide whether bikes may use a trail at all. A trail with a demanding hiking grade counts as a mountain-bike trail. A trail with a hiking grade or a faint line and no sign that bikes are allowed is closed. The panel shows both, where mapped[3].

Who may ride each level

Some trails stay closed to every ride type:

  • trails signed no bicycles, private trails, hiking-only trails and park paths closed to bikes;
  • dirt paths with no rating and no sign that bikes may use them. When it is unclear whether bikes are allowed, RouteMaker treats the way as closed.

Gravel and Mountain Goat also skip a Green trail mapped as closed when wet. "Keep to roads, not trails" takes any ride type off trails. The mountain-bike trail layer draws only trails a bike may use, and names them along the line from zoom 15. Major trails are named along the line in purple from zoom 12, the zoom every street appears at, and from zoom 15 every other path's name is purple too.

A dirt trail on other ride types

Default and the other ride types that skip dirt trails never plan onto one by themselves. But a stop, via point or end you put on a dirt trail is routed to along it, and along the dirt trails joined to it. Starting on one is not enough, as whether it is rideable depends on the weather. Once you are riding a dirt trail, every re-route on that ride may use any dirt trail. Pressing Road closed on a dirt trail during a ride keeps that ride's re-routes off dirt trails, even to a stop on one, until you plan again; a road that only crosses a dirt trail does not count. Rated singletrack (Green and up) stays closed to them. This is not on E-bike or Mass Ride, with electric assist or "Keep to roads, not trails" on, or yet on weekend rides.

Tapping a trail

Turn on Layers, Trails and terrain, Mountain-bike trails. Trails show from zoom 12. While they show, a small key under the zoom buttons lists each look: Unrated, 1 Green, 2 Blue, 3 Black and 4 Red. The key is not shown in Go; the Layers sheet has it there.

More: Tapping a trail
  • What it is like: the surface as mapped (dirt, rock, gravel), smoothness, width, how easy the trail is to follow, its climbing rating and its hiking scale, where mapped[3].
  • Difficulty: its level, or "Not rated: no difficulty is mapped for this trail."
  • Elevation profile: heights every 98 ft [30 m] along this section (further apart on a long one), from USGS elevation data[7]. It gives the length, the height gained and lost, and the steepest grade. Each end is marked with its compass direction (N ... S), and a screen reader hears it, such as "Runs north to south."
  • With a mouse, hovering a trail or a climb shows a small card, as a Metro station does: the trail's name, its difficulty and what that is based on, or the climb's length, gain and grade, with its elevation profile. A click opens the full panel.
  • Route through this trail: adds a via point on the trail. It is offered only when your ride type uses the trail. Otherwise the panel says which ride types do.
  • No traffic stress: a trail has no traffic, so its panel has no stress level and no way to suggest one.
  • Where MORE has a page for the trail system, the panel links to it[6].

The Climbs layer

The Climbs layer marks every categorized climb on the roads, paths and trails a bike may use, with a symbol at its top. The number in the symbol is the climb's category, from 5 (smallest) to 1, then HC (the biggest). The shape is how steep it is on average: a circle is gentle (under 4%), a square moderate (4% to 7.9%) and a diamond steep (8% or more). The color is what most of the climb is ridden on: a road, a paved path with no traffic, gravel or dirt, or a mountain-bike trail. Mountain Goat turns the layer on, and its climb search uses the same climbs.

More: The Climbs layer

The symbols in detail

  • Number: the category. 5 is a short rise you notice; 3 is a real mountain climb for this region.
  • Circle, square or diamond: the average grade over the whole climb. A long gentle climb and a short steep one can share a category, so the shape tells them apart.
  • Color by surface: what more than half the climb is ridden on. A mountain-bike trail is checked first, then gravel or dirt, then a paved path with no traffic. Anything else is a road. The color says what bike suits it; it never changes the category.
  • The dashed line from zoom 12 is the climb itself, from its start to the top, in the same color. Summit symbols show from zoom 9.

How a climb is named

From the next map update, each climb is named from OpenStreetMap[4] when the map is built: by its roads and, where it has one, the named hill or gap it climbs.

  • Roads: any named road (or route number, such as MD 77) that carries at least a quarter of the climb, in the order you ride them, at most two: "Snider Lane", "Hollow Road and Ridge Road".
  • The hill: a named gap, peak, hill or ridge the climb runs up, when its top is near the climb and the climb gets at least half way up to it. A gap wins over a peak, and a peak over a ridge.
  • Put together: "Foxville Road up Catoctin Mountain", or "Gapland Road to Crampton Gap" for a gap. The hill is left off when the road's name already says it.
  • Without a road name: "Sugarloaf Mountain climb", else "Climb near Thurmont" for the nearest town within 3 mi [5 km], else just its category, such as "Category 4 climb".

The climbs data built on October 12, 2026 has 3,750 climbs. Most are small: nine in ten are Category 5. The region's hills top out at Category 2; nothing reaches Category 1 or HC.

How a climb is found

  • Heights: every way is sampled every 98 ft [30 m] on USGS 1 arc-second elevation data[7]. Each way is read in both directions, and a climb against a one-way street is dropped.
  • Breaks, not tops: a short dip or a flat stretch does not end a climb. A dip ends it only past a fifth of the height gained so far (at least 33 ft [10 m], at most 165 ft [50 m]). A flat or downhill stretch ends it only past a quarter of the length so far (at least 0.25 mi [400 m], at most 1 mi [1.6 km]).
  • Gentle ends trimmed: a near-flat approach is cut off, so a short wall after a long flat is listed as the wall.
  • Minimums: at least 0.31 mi [500 m] long and at least 1% on average. Rail trails can count. Garmin's minimum grade is 3%.

How a climb is rated

Climb categories come from bike racing. The Tour de France ranks the climbs on each stage, from Category 4, the smallest, to Category 1, and above those "hors catégorie" (HC), French for beyond category[9]. Race organizers choose each climb's category by judgement. Strava and Garmin turned the idea into a formula, so that any climb anywhere can be categorized the same way, and RouteMaker uses their formula.

A climb's score is its length in meters times its average grade in percent. This is the scale Strava and Garmin use; RouteMaker adds a Category 5[8]. Length times grade comes to 100 times the height gained in meters, so the category depends on the net gain. A climb scoring under 4,000 is not a climb. For example, a climb of 1 mi [1.6 km] at 5% scores about 8,050: Category 4.

Which climbs show

  • Show climbs from: Category 4 by default, which shows Category 4, 3, 2, 1 and HC and hides Category 5. Category 5 climbs are many and small. Choose 5 to show every climb.
  • Hide climbs on busy roads: on by default. It hides a climb when more than half of it is on roads rated LTS 3 or higher. Paths and trails never count as busy.
  • A climb on a mountain-bike trail shows only while the map draws trails: with the trail layer on, or on Mountain Bike.
  • Tap a climb's marker or its line for a card with its profile and "Ride this climb".

Topo lines

Topo lines are contour lines over the map, from USGS 1/3 arc-second elevation[7]. They show from zoom 10. The spacing follows the zoom:

More: Topo lines
  • every 100 ft [30 m] up to zoom 12;
  • every 40 ft [12 m] at zoom 13;
  • every 20 ft [6 m] from zoom 14.

Every fifth line is a heavier index line, labeled in feet with meters in brackets. The brown look follows OpenTopoMap[10].
