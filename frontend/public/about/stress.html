How traffic stress ratings work

RouteMaker gives every road and path a traffic-stress rating. The rating is how stressful the traffic beside you is likely to feel on a bike. It colors the map, and it decides which roads a route prefers. This page explains where the ratings come from and how each ride type uses them.

The levels

The scale is Level of Traffic Stress (LTS), a widely used four-level scale for bike riding, plus a fifth level of RouteMaker's own called Avoid. Each level below gives the words the road panel uses, then the shorter words in the map legend. On the map, each level also has its own line width and dash pattern, so the levels can be told apart without color.

  • LTS 1: Comfortable for everyone. Map legend: "Comfortable for most people". Trails and paths, and very quiet, slow streets. On the map: a thin, solid light green line with a dark green edge.
  • LTS 2: Fine for adults. Map legend: "Comfortable for most adults". Quiet streets, and streets with a good bike lane. On the map: long green dashes with a dark blue edge.
  • LTS 3: For experienced cyclists. Map legend: "For confident riders". Busier or faster streets. On the map: short yellow dashes on orange, with a black edge.
  • LTS 4: High stress: busy, fast traffic. Map legend: "Heavy or fast traffic". Wide or fast roads. On the map: very long orange dashes on red, with a black edge, in a wider line.
  • Avoid. Map legend: "Legal, but best avoided". You may legally ride it, but it is not a place to send anyone, such as an expressway. On the map: red dashes and dots on black, the widest line.

The colors here are the map's default ones. The High contrast switch, in Map layers and in Settings, changes the map to colors that don't rely on red and green, and draws the lines a little bolder. The dash patterns stay the same.

How a road gets its rating

RouteMaker rates each road itself, using the published LTS method (Furth's tables). It rates the same way on both sides of every county and state line. It looks at these, in this order:

  1. Speed. The posted speed limit. Where none is mapped, it uses the legal default: 20 mph [32 km/h] in the District, and the Maryland and Virginia defaults elsewhere. Where in doubt it assumes the faster figure.
  2. Lanes. More lanes in each direction means more stress.
  3. Bike facilities. A separated bike lane rates calmest. A painted lane or a wide paved shoulder helps on slower roads, and less on fast ones.
  4. Traffic volume. Vehicle counts from the District, Virginia and agency street records. On a two-lane road with no bike lane, very light traffic lowers the rating one level, and heavy traffic raises it one level. Sometimes one block's count is far above the rest of its street, often because traffic bunches at an intersection. Then the street's typical count is used for the road, and the higher count still counts at the intersection.
  5. Road type. The classifier does not rate a main road below "For experienced cyclists" unless it has a bike facility. It does not rate a minor through road below "Fine for adults". A correction can still change either (see Corrections).
  6. Surface. A rough surface, such as dirt or grass, keeps a road out of the calmest level.

In the District, the city's own street records (posted speed, lanes, one-way, bike lanes and parking) take priority over the map's tags. A small number of roads have a rating set by hand (see Corrections).

Ratings are updated with each weekly map data update.

Avoid, and roads closed to bikes

Avoid roads are legal for bikes. Routes stay off them unless there is no reasonable other way. Every ride type adds a charge each time a route enters an Avoid road. On Default, the charge is about as much as 2.5 extra miles [4 km] of quiet street.

Closed to bikes is different. It means you may not ride there at all, and routes do not use it. Examples are roads and paths inside military bases and secured government sites, ways signed no bicycles, and rated mountain-bike singletrack. When it is unclear whether bikes are allowed, RouteMaker treats the way as closed. A closed way can be reopened by hand when there is evidence that bikes may use it.

Other natural-surface mountain-bike trails are closed on most ride types. They are open on Gravel and Mountain Goat.

Corrections

Map data is not always right. RouteMaker's owner can change a road's rating up or down, and can set a minimum ("at least this level"). Reasons include fast drivers, poor sightlines, a dangerous merge, or one road being the better choice among similar ones. Most corrections are hidden. Where a correction has a published note, a route that uses that road says why. Notes describe the road and its traffic, never a neighborhood or its people.

Corrections are never made automatically. Each one is reviewed and approved by the owner, and takes effect at the next map data update.

Planned: a way for signed-in riders to suggest a rating from the road panel. Suggestions will be reviewed and never applied automatically.

How ride types and the slider use the ratings

A route is chosen by adding up the cost of every stretch it rides. The yardstick is a quiet street: a mile of quiet street counts as 1 calm mile. A busier stretch costs more, as if it were longer.

Their level adds nothing at LTS 1 or 2, except on Trailmaxxing, Cargo with passengers and Riding with kids, where low-stress streets (LTS 2) count a little extra. A quiet street counts as 1. Traffic-free paths and protected bike lanes count for less, and painted bike lanes a little less (except on Mass Ride), so routes lean toward them.

The traffic-stress slider changes these figures:

  • Toward calm: busy roads count for more.
  • Near the top: RouteMaker also searches for calmer routes itself, and will add real distance to avoid busy roads.
  • At the top: routes are ranked by the least LTS 4 and very high stress junctions first. Next comes the least LTS 3 and higher stress junctions, then distance. A longer route wins only when the extra miles buy a real cut in stress: about 1 mile [1.6 km] of LTS 3 avoided for each 5 miles [8 km] added. LTS 4 and Avoid count for more.
  • Trailmaxxing, Cargo Bike with passengers and Riding with kids never pick a route with more LTS 4, Avoid and very high stress crossings, counted together, than the router's own first route.
  • When RouteMaker searches for calmer routes on Trailmaxxing and Cargo Bike with passengers, a mile of LTS 2 also counts as a quarter of a mile of LTS 3, so those routes lean toward LTS 1 streets.
  • Riding with kids counts a mile of LTS 2 as half a mile of LTS 3, so it strongly prefers paths and LTS 1 streets.
  • On every ride type except Mass Ride, you can move the slider from where the ride type starts it.
  • A route will never get busier just because you asked it to avoid hills.

Junction warnings

Crossings and turns have a cost of their own, measured as the extra distance of quiet street they are worth. Crossing a busy road (LTS 3) from a stop sign costs about 1,200 feet [370 m]. Crossing an LTS 4 road from a stop sign costs about 3,000 feet [910 m]. A signal makes crossings much cheaper (150 to 300 feet [45 to 90 m]). Left turns cost more than right turns. Faster, wider or busier roads, and rural stop signs, cost more again. Quiet streets meeting quiet streets cost almost nothing.

  • Higher stress junction (orange marker): a cost of 600 feet [180 m] or more.
  • Very high stress junction (red marker): 2,000 feet [610 m] or more.

Each warning in the route description describes the road, the movement and the control, in words.

The stress chart

Under a route's elevation chart, a second chart shows how stressful the route is along its length, so short busy stretches and bad crossings stand out. It is on every ride type except Mass Ride.

The chart shows calm miles per mile (a calm mile is explained under How ride types and the slider use the ratings). Each road counts what the routing charges for its traffic, by its own speed, lanes and bike lane. About 1 means all quiet streets. Traffic-free paths and protected bike lanes count for less, painted bike lanes a little less, and busier and faster roads for more. Each point is worked out over the mile [1.6 km] around it, or less near the start and end. Crossings count too: a crossing's cost is added to every point within half a mile [0.8 km] of it. So a quiet street that crosses many busy roads can read higher than a straight, busier one.

  • The scale is squeezed toward the top, so a stretch at 1.4 and a spike at 14 both show. 1 to 2 takes as much height as 5 to 10.
  • The area under the line is colored like the map: low stress, LTS 3 level, and LTS 4 level or higher, with the same patterns as the stress bar. Dotted lines mark where the color changes, for example "LTS 3 from 2.7" and "LTS 4 from 9.3" on the Default ride type at its starting slider. These change with the ride type and the slider.
  • A dashed line shows each stretch's own figure, so a short busy stretch still shows at its true level.
  • Avoid stretches are magenta blocks marked "AVOID", or "A" where short. Just above the stress chart, an orange triangle marks a higher stress junction and a red diamond a very high stress junction. Other crossings count in the line but have no marker.

Without the picture: the chart is a slider named "Elevation and rolling stress along the route". Tab to it and use the arrow keys to move along the route. Page Up and Page Down move further, Home and End go to the ends, and C goes to the next climb. At each point it says the mile, the elevation and grade, the stress of the stretch you are on, and how the mile around reads, for example "1.4 calm miles per mile, LTS 1 to 2 level". It then gives the next junction to watch. The summary above the chart gives the route's total in calm miles (with calm kilometers), the average, and the most stressful mile and what is in it. Under the chart, a section you can open, "Climbs and rolling stress as tables", lists the figure every half mile [0.8 km] on shorter routes, and less often on longer ones.

If the router could not give a road's speed and lanes, that road is priced by its stress level instead, and the chart's key then calls the figure an estimate.

Mass Ride

Mass Ride plans for a large group that takes the whole lane, so traffic stress hardly matters to it. Its slider is fixed at the most direct end. LTS 3 and LTS 4 cost nothing extra, and only Avoid roads are charged. Its map shows how many riders a road can carry each minute instead of stress levels, in the District only. Avoid roads show the word "AVOID". Its junction markers are orange for a crossing of an LTS 3 road and red for LTS 4 or Avoid. Under its elevation chart it shows riders per minute instead of the stress chart.

Half steps and crossing costs (planned)

Planned, not built yet. These are decided in outline and are not in the app today.

Half steps

Each road's level is a number, 1 to 5 (5 is Avoid). Some roads sit between two levels. Half steps will add 1.5 "Probably comfortable for older children", 2.5 "Fine for most confident adults", 3.5 "Stressful even for experienced cyclists" and 4.5 "A very high-stress road". A half step will cost halfway between its two levels, in routing and in the stress chart. 4.5 is never treated as Avoid. On the map, a half step will look like the level below it. Zoomed in, it will add a dash in the color of the level above.

Crossing costs

The costs of crossings will also be revised. The hardest crossings will cost more, and changing lanes to turn left will have a cost of its own. Crossings will also cost a little more at weekday rush hours, and less at night and on weekend days. These are planned, and the numbers are not final.

Why our ratings differ from the studies

The levels come from the Level of Traffic Stress method (Mekuria, Furth and Nixon, 2012, and Furth's later versions), Montgomery County's own ratings, and studies of where cyclists in Portland, Eugene and Copenhagen choose to ride. The sources are listed under Sources. We differ on purpose in a few places. We add an Avoid level. We treat a road as closed to bicycles when it is unclear whether they are allowed. We steer most people toward trails and calm streets more than the studies suggest. The planned crossing costs are our own choices, set at the top of the published ranges, and higher for the worst crossings at the owner's direction. We also chose the size of the planned rush-hour change ourselves, because the one study behind it is small. The full list, with a link to each source and the reason for each difference, is on the page Traffic stress: our sources and where we differ (on GitHub).

Reporting a wrong rating

  • The map data is wrong or missing (a speed limit, a bike lane, the number of lanes): use Edit in OSM in the road panel to fix it in OpenStreetMap. It needs a free OpenStreetMap account. Do not copy from Google Street View. The rating follows at a later map data update.
  • The data is right but the rating feels wrong: tell the person who gave you access to RouteMaker. If the beta notice shows a "Report a problem" link, use that. Include the page's address (it holds your route), the road, and what you expected.
