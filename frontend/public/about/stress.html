How traffic stress ratings work

RouteMaker gives every road and path a traffic-stress rating. The rating is how stressful the traffic beside you is likely to feel on a bike. It colors the map, and it decides which roads a route prefers. This page explains where the ratings come from and how each ride type uses them.

At a glance

  • Six levels: LTS 1, which suits children, to LTS 4 for the most traffic-tolerant riders, then 4.5 and Avoid for the worst roads, with half steps between.
  • The first source that rates a road wins: community corrections, then a local agency, then the state agency, then RouteMaker's own formula.
  • The level colors the map and sets what a road costs a route. Hills, pavement and scenery never change it.
  • Riders can suggest a level from the road panel; every suggestion is reviewed by hand.

Looking for something else? How the levels become a route: Routing. What each ride type accepts: Ride types.

The levels

The scale is Level of Traffic Stress (LTS), a widely used four-level scale for bike riding, plus a fifth level of RouteMaker's own called Avoid. Each level below gives the words the road panel and the map legend use. On the map, each level also has its own line width and dash pattern, so the levels can be told apart without color.

More: The levels
  • LTS 1: Comfortable for everyone. Trails and paths, and very quiet, slow streets. On the map: a thin, solid light green line with a dark green edge.
  • LTS 2: Fine for adults. Quiet streets, and streets with a good bike lane. On the map: long green dashes with a dark blue edge.
  • LTS 3: For experienced cyclists. Busier or faster streets. On the map: short yellow dashes on orange, with a black edge.
  • LTS 4: High stress: busy, fast traffic. Wide or fast roads. On the map: very long orange dashes on red, with a black edge, in a wider line.
  • Avoid. You may legally ride it, but it is not a place to send anyone, such as an expressway in a city or suburb. A fast expressway in the countryside is rated LTS 4.5 instead: high stress, but often there is no way around it. On the map: red dashes and dots on black, the widest line.

The colors here are the map's default ones. The High contrast switch, in Map layers and in Settings, changes the map to colors that don't rely on red and green, draws the lines a little bolder, and makes the street names darker. The dash patterns stay the same.

How a road gets its rating

A road's level comes from the first of these that rates it: an approved correction from the community (see Corrections); a local agency's rating (Montgomery County Planning, or DDOT in the District); a state agency's rating (Maryland DOT State Highway Administration); and otherwise RouteMaker's own formula. From the next map update, Montgomery County's level 5 is read as LTS 4.5, and a road Maryland rates 5 (bicycles prohibited) is closed to bikes; until then both are Avoid. The page Which rating wins explains the order, with a diagram.

More: How a road gets its rating

The formula uses the published LTS method (Furth's tables). It rates the same way on both sides of every county and state line. It looks at these, in this order:

  1. Speed. The posted speed limit, and in the City of Alexandria the limit the city records for the street. Where none is mapped, it uses the legal default in the District, 20 mph [32 km/h]. In Maryland's and Virginia's towns and cities it uses Maryland DOT's estimates by road type, 25 to 35 mph [40 to 56 km/h], and outside them 50 to 55 mph [80 to 89 km/h] on through roads. Where in doubt it assumes the faster figure. Each state's legal default limits are used only for junction costs (see Junction warnings).
  2. Lanes. More lanes in each direction means more stress.
  3. Bike facilities. A separated bike lane rates calmest. A painted lane or a wide paved shoulder helps on slower roads, and less on fast ones.
  4. Traffic volume. Vehicle counts from the District, Virginia and agency street records. On a two-lane road with no bike lane, very light traffic lowers the rating one level, and heavy traffic raises it one level. Sometimes one block's count is far above the rest of its street, often because traffic bunches at an intersection. Then the street's typical count is used for the road, and the higher count still counts at the intersection.
  5. Road type. The classifier does not rate a main road below "For experienced cyclists" unless it has a bike facility. It does not rate a minor through road below "Fine for adults". A correction can still change either (see Corrections).
  6. Surface. A rough surface, such as dirt or grass, keeps a road out of the calmest level.

In the District, the city's own street records (posted speed, lanes, one-way, bike lanes and parking) take priority over the map's tags. A small number of roads have a rating set by hand (see Corrections).

Ratings are updated with each weekly map data update.

Which rating wins

Several sources can rate the same road. RouteMaker uses the first one that does, in this order:

More: Which rating wins
  1. Community input. Corrections from riders and from RouteMaker's owner, for reasons such as fast drivers, poor sightlines or a dangerous merge. Each one is approved by hand; none is applied automatically.
  2. Local agency ratings. Montgomery County Planning's Bicycle Level of Traffic Stress[1] and DDOT's Bicycle Level of Traffic Stress in the District[2]. A county knows its roads better than a state does.
  3. State agency ratings. Maryland DOT State Highway Administration's statewide ratings[3], read from its adjusted score.
  4. The LTS formula. RouteMaker's own rating from OpenStreetMap[4] and street facts, described below. It rates every road the same way on both sides of every county and state line.

Why the local agency wins

Inside Montgomery County both the county and the state rate many of the same roads. They mostly agree, but where they differ the county is usually the stricter one, and usually closer to how the road feels:

  • Rural roads upcounty (about 120 mi [190 km]), such as Peach Tree, Sugarland and Comus Roads: Maryland's formula scores an unstriped, low-traffic road LTS 1; the county rates many of them LTS 3 or 4. RouteMaker's own rural floor would not call them LTS 1 either.
  • Busy arterials (about 72 mi [116 km]), such as Darnestown Road, Georgia Avenue and Great Seneca Highway: Maryland says LTS 4; the county says 5, which RouteMaker reads as 4.5 from the next map update.
  • Where Maryland is stricter, as on Beach Drive (Maryland 4, the county 2), the county's rating still wins, and both show in the road panel.

Test cases

The roads where the two agencies disagree most are kept as test cases. Whenever the ratings or the matching change, these are the roads checked first: a wide gap is either a real difference in method, which the table explains, or a sign that two different roads were matched. Only 5.6 mi [9.0 km] of road differ by three levels (Maryland 2, the county 5), and most of that is matching, not method. Inside the county the county's level is used in every case.

What each place tells us

Every jurisdiction publishes different data, so the evidence behind a rating changes when you cross a line. The District publishes the most: its own stress ratings, street-by-street records of speed, lanes and bike lanes, traffic counts, pavement condition and its own closure feeds. Montgomery County publishes its own ratings. The rest of Maryland has the state's ratings on state roads only. Virginia publishes no stress ratings RouteMaker can use, so every Virginia road is rated by the formula from OpenStreetMap and traffic counts.

The formula in detail

Where no correction or agency rates a road, RouteMaker rates it from the published LTS tables[13], written fresh from the tables rather than copied from another tool:

More: The formula in detail

Where no speed limit is mapped, "town" means inside a 2020 Census urban area[14], and the District's default is its legal 20 mph [32 km/h][10]. An unpaved rural road is read at no more than 30 mph [48 km/h], and a narrow or winding rural road with no traffic count at 25 mph [40 km/h].

A paved shoulder at least 4 ft [1.2 m] wide is read like a bike lane, and never worse than having none. On a road with one lane each way and no bike facility, traffic counts[6] move the level: 1,500 vehicles a day or fewer lowers it one level, and 8,000 or more raises it one level. Without a bike facility, trunk, primary and secondary roads are at least LTS 3, and tertiary roads at least LTS 2.

Special cases

A few kinds of road get a rule of their own: rural roads are never LTS 1, rural expressways are LTS 4.5, motorways and some side-path roads are Avoid, and some DC park paths are open to bikes.

More: Special cases
  • Rural roads are at least LTS 2. LTS 1 is meant for children, and a rural road open to cars never qualifies, however quiet. Trails and roads closed to cars are not affected.
  • Quiet rural roads are not overrated. RouteMaker does not push a lightly used rural or gravel road to high stress on a default speed limit alone; it lowers a rating only for a clear reason, such as agency data or what riders report.
  • Rural expressways are LTS 4.5. A posted 50 mph [80 km/h] or faster expressway outside a town is very high stress, but often there is no way around it. The same road in a town is Avoid.
  • Motorways are closed to bikes unless signed open. One signed open to bikes is Avoid.
  • "Use the side path" roads (OpenStreetMap's bicycle=use_sidepath) are Avoid only where the side path is actually mapped beside them; otherwise they are rated like any other road.
  • Parking aisles and driveways open to bikes are treated as ordinary service roads and usable for routing. From the next map update they are drawn on the map too.
  • DC park paths. Paths in DC's circle parks (Dupont, Logan and others) are open to bikes, and sidewalks on National Park Service land in the Central Business District and on the Capitol grounds are exempt from the District's sidewalk ban. At memorials where you must walk, such as the Martin Luther King Jr. and Franklin Delano Roosevelt memorials, routes take you there and you walk the last bit.

What never changes a rating

Scenery, pavement, hills, hazard reports and junction costs can change what a route costs, but never a road's stress level.

More: What never changes a rating
  • Scenic roads. The Scenic layer (Montgomery County Rustic Roads, Maryland Scenic Byways, the East Coast Greenway, U.S. Bicycle Routes, covered bridges) is off by default and never changes a rating or a route.
  • Pavement. DC's Pavement Condition Index[9] makes a rough DC street cost a little more to ride; it never changes the street's stress level.
  • Hills. The Hills slider and the climb layer change effort, not stress.
  • Hazard reports on Mass Ride appear only on the Mass Ride map, after review.
  • Junction costs are added when a route is planned (see Routing); they never change a road's level.

Avoid, and roads closed to bikes

Avoid roads are legal for bikes. Routes stay off them unless there is no reasonable other way. Every ride type adds a charge each time a route enters an Avoid road. On Default, the charge is about as much as 2.5 extra miles [4 km] of quiet street.

More: Avoid, and roads closed to bikes

Closed to bikes is different. It means you may not ride there at all, and routes do not use it. Examples are roads and paths inside military bases and secured government sites, and ways signed no bicycles. When it is unclear whether bikes are allowed, RouteMaker treats the way as closed. A closed way can be reopened by hand when there is evidence that bikes may use it.

Sidewalks are the exception, from the next map update. A sidewalk or crosswalk with no bike tag is ridden as a last resort. Beside a road rated 4.5 or Avoid it counts as LTS 3, and a route takes it over that road from the traffic slider's middle up. Beside a calmer road, LTS 4 included, the road is taken, and the sidewalk counts as LTS 4 and is used only where nothing else connects. Where a bike path stops a few steps short of a bike lane, side path or street, the short footway between them counts as LTS 3. A crosswalk over a slip lane adds a wait to the route's time. DC's downtown sidewalks stay closed, and escalators are closed to bikes, so routes take the elevator.

Mountain-bike trails are closed on most ride types. Mountain Bike rides all of them. Gravel and Mountain Goat ride the ones with no rating, and from the next map update the green (level 1) ones too. A trail signed no bicycles stays closed on every ride type. Default and the other ride types never plan onto an unrated dirt trail, but one you are on, or put a stop or via point on, is routed along, with the dirt trails joined to it (not yet on weekend rides).

Cobblestones and rails in the road

Some roads have a known hazard: cobblestones (such as Georgetown's O and P Streets NW), or rails set in the road (such as the old DC Streetcar tracks on H Street NE). From the next map update, routes price these roads as heavily as a very busy road, so they use one only when the way round is as bad. The road keeps its own stress rating. Mass Ride is the exception: it takes the big roads by design. Paths aren't priced either: a path with a hazard is marked when a route uses it, not avoided.

More: Cobblestones and rails in the road

From then, when a route does ride one, an amber triangle marks it on the route, the directions name it, and Ride warns you ahead. The rest of the map does not show them. Hazards come from OpenStreetMap, plus a short reviewed list for ones it misses.

Corrections

Map data is not always right. RouteMaker's owner can change a road's rating up or down, and can set a minimum ("at least this level"). Reasons include fast drivers, poor sightlines, a dangerous merge, or one road being the better choice among similar ones. Most corrections are hidden. Where a correction has a published note, a route that uses that road says why. Notes describe the road and its traffic, never a neighborhood or its people.

More: Corrections

Corrections are never made automatically. Each one is reviewed and approved by the owner, and takes effect at the next map data update.

Signed-in riders can suggest a rating from the road panel, with Suggest a change. Suggestions are reviewed and never applied automatically.

How ride types and the slider use the ratings

A route is chosen by adding up the cost of every stretch it rides. The yardstick is a quiet street: a mile of quiet street counts as 1 calm mile. A busier stretch costs more, as if it were longer.

More: How ride types and the slider use the ratings

Their level adds nothing at LTS 1 or 2, except on Trailmaxxing, Cargo with passengers and Novice and Nervous, where low-stress streets (LTS 2) count a little extra. A quiet street counts as 1. Traffic-free paths and protected bike lanes count for less, and painted bike lanes a little less (except on Mass Ride), so routes lean toward them.

The table shows how many calm miles one mile of each busier level counts as. Each figure is a range, because faster and wider roads of the same level cost more.

The traffic-stress slider changes these figures:

  • Toward calm: busy roads count for more.
  • Near the top: RouteMaker also searches for calmer routes itself, and will add real distance to avoid busy roads.
  • At the top: routes are ranked by the least LTS 4 and very high stress junctions first. Next comes the least LTS 3 and higher stress junctions, then distance. A longer route wins only when the extra miles buy a real cut in stress: about 1 mile [1.6 km] of LTS 3 avoided for each 5 miles [8 km] added. LTS 4 and Avoid count for more.
  • Trailmaxxing, Cargo Bike with passengers and Novice and Nervous never pick a route with more LTS 4, Avoid and very high stress crossings, counted together, than the router's own first route.
  • On Trailmaxxing and Cargo Bike with passengers, a mile of LTS 2 also costs a quarter of what a mile of LTS 3 adds, so those routes lean toward LTS 1 streets. Like LTS 3's extra cost, it fades to nothing at the slider's busiest end.
  • Novice and Nervous reads every road and junction one level busier than it is, so it strongly prefers paths and LTS 1 streets, with shorter detours than Trailmaxxing.
  • On every ride type except Mass Ride, you can move the slider from where the ride type starts it.
  • A route will never get busier just because you asked it to avoid hills.

Junction warnings

Crossings and turns have a cost of their own, in calm miles: the extra distance of quiet street they are worth. Crossing a busy road (LTS 3) from a stop sign costs about 0.30 calm miles [0.49 km; 1,600 ft]. Crossing an LTS 4 road from a stop sign costs about 0.76 calm miles [1.22 km; 4,000 ft]. A signal makes crossings much cheaper (0.03 to 0.11 calm miles [0.05 to 0.18 km; 150 to 600 ft]). Left turns cost more than right turns, and changing lanes to reach a left turn costs more on busier and faster roads. At a signal, a left turn never costs more than a two-stage turn, 0.14 calm miles [0.23 km; 750 ft]. Faster, wider or busier roads, and rural stop signs, cost more again. The hardest junctions, on LTS 4 roads with no signal, can cost up to 2 calm miles [3.22 km; 10,560 ft]. Quiet streets meeting quiet streets cost almost nothing.

More: Junction warnings

Where no speed limit is mapped, junction costs use the legal default limit of the District, Maryland or Virginia (see Sources).

  • Higher stress junction (orange marker): a cost of 0.15 calm miles [0.24 km; 800 ft] or more.
  • Very high stress junction (red marker): 0.59 calm miles [0.94 km; 3,100 ft] or more.

Each warning in the route description describes the road, the movement and the control, in words.

The ride time you choose matters too. Junctions with a busy road count for more at weekday rush hours (7 to 10 AM and 4 to 7 PM), a little less on weekend days, and less at night (9 PM to 7 AM, every day). Traffic is usually calmer at night, but not always.

Each warning in the route's junction list, and the card a marker opens, gives that junction's own cost in calm miles, for example "Costs 0.74 calm mi (1.19 calm km), more at rush hour". The words after the figure appear only where the ride time changed it.

The stress chart

Under a route's elevation chart, a second chart shows how stressful the route is along its length, so short busy stretches and bad crossings stand out. It is on every ride type except Mass Ride.

More: The stress chart

The chart shows calm miles per mile (a calm mile is explained under How ride types and the slider use the ratings). Each road counts what the routing charges for its traffic, by its own speed, lanes and bike lane. About 1 means all quiet streets. Traffic-free paths and protected bike lanes count for less, painted bike lanes a little less, and busier and faster roads for more. Each point is worked out over the mile [1.6 km] around it, or less near the start and end. Crossings count too: a crossing's cost is added to every point within half a mile [0.8 km] of it. So a quiet street that crosses many busy roads can read higher than a straight, busier one.

  • The scale is squeezed toward the top, so a stretch at 1.4 and a spike at 14 both show. 1 to 2 takes as much height as 5 to 10.
  • The area under the line is colored like the map: low stress, LTS 3 level, and LTS 4 level or higher, with the same patterns as the stress bar. Dotted lines mark where the color changes, for example "LTS 3 from 2.7" and "LTS 4 from 9.3" on the Default ride type at its starting slider. These change with the ride type and the slider.
  • A dashed line shows each stretch's own figure, so a short busy stretch still shows at its true level.
  • Avoid stretches are magenta blocks marked "AVOID", or "A" where short. Just above the stress chart, an orange triangle marks a higher stress junction and a red diamond a very high stress junction. Other crossings count in the line but have no marker.

Without the picture: the chart is a slider named "Elevation and rolling stress along the route". Tab to it and use the arrow keys to move along the route. Page Up and Page Down move further, Home and End go to the ends, and C goes to the next climb. At each point it says the mile, the elevation and grade, the stress of the stretch you are on, and how the mile around reads, for example "1.4 calm miles per mile, LTS 1 to 2 level". It then gives the next junction to watch. The summary above the chart gives the route's total in calm miles (with calm kilometers), the average, and the most stressful mile and what is in it. Under the chart, a section you can open, "Climbs and rolling stress as tables", lists the figure every half mile [0.8 km] on shorter routes, and less often on longer ones.

If the router could not give a road's speed and lanes, that road is priced by its stress level instead, and the chart's key then calls the figure an estimate.

Mass Ride

Mass Ride plans for a large group that takes the whole lane, so traffic stress hardly matters to it. Its slider is fixed at the most direct end. LTS 3 and LTS 4 cost nothing extra, and only Avoid roads are charged. Its map shows how many riders a road can carry each minute instead of stress levels, in the District only. Avoid roads show the word "AVOID". Its junction markers are orange for a crossing of an LTS 3 road and red for LTS 4 or Avoid. Above its elevation chart it shows riders per minute and the corker load instead of the stress chart. Mass Ride explains its method.

Half steps

Each road's level is a number, 1 to 5 (5 is Avoid). Some roads sit between two levels. Half steps are 1.5 "Probably comfortable for older children", 2.5 "Fine for most confident adults", 3.5 "Stressful even for experienced cyclists" and 4.5 "A very high-stress road". A half step costs halfway between its two levels, in routing and in the stress chart. 4.5 is never treated as Avoid. On the map, a half step looks like the level below it. From zoom 15 it adds a dash in the color of the level above.

Why our ratings differ from the studies

The levels come from the Level of Traffic Stress method (Mekuria, Furth and Nixon, 2012, and Furth's later versions), Montgomery County's own ratings, and studies of where cyclists in Portland, Eugene and Copenhagen choose to ride. The sources are listed under Sources. We differ on purpose in a few places. We add an Avoid level. We treat a road as closed to bicycles when it is unclear whether they are allowed. We steer most people toward trails and calm streets more than the studies suggest. Our crossing costs are our own choices, set at the top of the published ranges, and higher for the worst crossings at the owner's direction. We also chose the size of the rush-hour change ourselves, because the one study behind it is small. The change at night is our own judgment. The full list, with a link to each source and the reason for each difference, is on the page Traffic stress: our sources and where we differ (on GitHub).

Reporting a wrong rating

If the map data is wrong, fix it in OpenStreetMap; if the data is right but the rating feels wrong, suggest a change from the road panel.

More: Reporting a wrong rating
  • The map data is wrong or missing (a speed limit, a bike lane, the number of lanes): use Edit in OSM in the road panel to fix it in OpenStreetMap. It needs a free OpenStreetMap account. Do not copy from Google Street View. The rating follows at a later map data update.
  • The data is right but the rating feels wrong: if you are signed in, use Suggest a change in the road panel. Otherwise, tell the person who gave you access to RouteMaker. If the beta notice shows a "Report a problem" link, use that. Include the page's address (it holds your route), the road, and what you expected.
