Mass Ride

Mass Ride is RouteMaker's ride type for a big group ride, such as a Critical Mass or a DC Bike Party. A group of hundreds takes a whole lane, so it plans in a different way from every other ride type. The question is not "how stressful is this road?" but "how many riders can this road carry each minute, and how long will the group hold each crossing?"

At a glance

  • A mass ride is a queueing problem, not a traffic-stress problem. The narrowest point and the number of crossings to hold decide how it goes.
  • The headline figure is riders per minute: about 100 for each 11 ft [3.35 m] lane.
  • Only the ride's own side of a two-way street counts. Parked cars and door zones do not.
  • Three charts follow the route: riders per minute, corker load and elevation.
  • The figures in this version are for the District of Columbia only.
  • The evidence is thin. No study of a real mass ride was found. The model rests on the owner's video counts and agrees with published bicycle flow studies. Treat it as about ±25%.

Looking for something else? How every other ride type plans: Routing. All ride types compared: Ride types.

What Mass Ride is for

Mass Ride is for rides of about 100 to 2,000 people that ride together through the city at a slow, social pace. Critical Mass and DC Bike Party are examples. The group stays together. If the light is red when the front arrives, the ride usually stops. Once the front goes through on green, the whole group follows, even after the light changes. Volunteers called corkers stand at busy junctions and hold cross traffic until the last rider has passed.

More: What Mass Ride is for

Every other ride type asks how stressful each road is for one rider in traffic: the Level of Traffic Stress (LTS). A big group fills the lane, so drivers wait behind it. What matters instead is capacity, pinch points, busy crossings, corkers and hills.

The figures in this version are for the District of Columbia only. Outside DC the route still plans, but the route list says "Outside DC: no figures yet". For a short summary beside the other ride types, see Group ride types.

What our research found

Mass Ride is built on RouteMaker's own research, the white paper Group Riding Flow and Mass Ride Planning (October 2026)[1]. Its findings in brief:

More: What our research found
  • A mass ride is governed by the narrowest point on the route and the number of crossings the group must hold, not by the stress of single streets.
  • Controlled experiments give a capacity for narrow paths, a drop in capacity once a queue forms, and a range of densities where flow holds steady.
  • Arithmetic on relaxed group spacing gives roughly 50 to 120 riders a minute per 11 ft [3.4 m] lane at 6 to 8 mph.
  • The owner's video counts of DC rides show more riders a minute, and tighter packing, than that relaxed spacing. They are indicative only.
  • No peer-reviewed study of corking, group cohesion or how fast a mass ride clears a crossing was found. The only published marshal ratio is one leader per 29 riders.

The paper grades every figure, and this page keeps those grades:

  • PRIMARY: read in the source itself.
  • SECONDARY: from a summary or a third-party document.
  • DERIVED: arithmetic on published figures.
  • JUDGEMENT: a recommendation with no direct source.

The app was built from the paper. It uses the owner's counts in place of the paper's relaxed spacing, so some of its figures differ. Each section below says what the paper found, then what the app does.

Flow capacity

More: Flow capacity

What the paper found

  • On narrow paths, 1.6 to 4.9 ft [0.5 to 1.5 m] wide, free-flow capacity is about 0.475 + 1.11 × width (in meters) riders a second. Once a queue forms, capacity drops by about 0.45 riders a second, about 27 a minute (PRIMARY)[14]. The formula should not be stretched to wider roads without a check.
  • On a 10 ft [3 m] wide track, flow stays roughly level between one rider per 63 ft² and one per 22 ft² [0.17 to 0.50 per m²] as riders form extra side-by-side lines. Looser is free flow; denser brings stop-and-go waves[12].
  • An often-quoted jam density of one rider per 15 ft² [0.7 per m²] and a speed of 11.8 mph [19 km/h] were design assumptions of an experiment, not results[20]. Riders on the wide track went at most about 9.3 mph [15 km/h][12]. Both are faster than a social mass ride.
  • At 6 to 8 mph and a relaxed spacing of one rider per 63 to 108 ft² [0.10 to 0.17 per m²], the paper works out the figures below (DERIVED). It suggests about 90 a minute per lane as a central value.

What the app does

RouteMaker's figure is the flow of a bunched pack, as the owner measured it on video at a turn. That is the state that limits a ride: at a pinch, a turn or a signal queue. In words:

riders a minute = usable width × 0.7 × density × pace × 60

  • Usable width: the road the ride has in its own direction (see Usable width).
  • 0.7: a pack bunches toward part of the road. It does not fill it edge to edge.
  • Density: one rider per 29 ft² [2.7 m², 0.37 riders per m²].
  • Pace: 4.3 mph [1.9 m/s].

The same riders pass each second whether the group is bunched or spread out. So on a straight at the cruising pace of 6 to 8 mph the group just spreads out, to about one rider per 48 ft² [4.5 m²] at 7 mph. The road is judged by what its pinches let through[4]. A road with two 11 ft lanes in the ride's direction carries about 200 riders a minute; one lane, about 100. The app's 100 sits inside the paper's 50 to 120, a little above its central 90. The app takes no separate capacity drop at a queue, because its figure already describes the bunched state[2].

Density and group length

More: Density and group length

What the paper found

At one rider per 5.5 to 10 ft of an 11 ft lane, a 1,000-rider group is about 1.0 to 1.9 mi [1.7 to 3.0 km] long on one lane, and half that on two. A 200-rider group is 0.2 to 0.4 mi [0.34 to 0.61 km]. Even fully jammed at the assumed one rider per 15 ft², 1,000 riders on one lane would fill about 0.27 mi [0.43 km] (all DERIVED).

What the app does

The Anticipated ride size slider runs from 100 to 2,000 riders, in steps of 50. It starts at 500. It sets the group's length, which drives the corker load and the requirement line on the riders chart. It does not change the route[5].

The group's length is the riders, divided by the riders a second the road carries, times the 7 mph [11 km/h] cruise. So the group is shorter on a wide avenue and longer on a narrow street. Where no width is known, the route's typical figure stands in, or two 11 ft lanes. On one lane, 1,000 riders come to about 1.18 mi [1.9 km]: inside the paper's range.

How a big group behaves

The evidence on how a group stays together is thin. Models of racing pelotons show that when riders put out little power, groups are loose and gappy rather than stretched into single file[21]. That is the state of a social mass ride, where spreading and gaps are the problem. The owner's videos agree in one way: packing followed speed more than turnout, and the slowest pack was the densest[3].

More: How a big group behaves

The paper's advice (JUDGEMENT):

  • Avoid single-lane pinches such as sidepaths, bridge sidewalks and trail gates. If one cannot be avoided, regroup before it, not at it, so the group enters at free flow.
  • Hold as few crossings as you can, and prefer two-lane roads.
  • Add regroup points after long or hilly sections.

What the app does: Mass Ride routes on roads only, so sidepaths, trails and trail gates are left out. The map and the riders chart show where a route narrows. RouteMaker does not place regroup points itself; plan a regroup as a stop.

Corking and marshals

Corkers are riders who block cross traffic at a busy junction so the whole ride passes together. RouteMaker finds the junctions that need them, works out how many each needs and how many are held at once, and gives the ride a headline of about twice that peak, since corkers leapfrog ahead.

More: Corking and marshals

What the paper found

Corking is well described by riders and journalists: a few riders block cross traffic so the mass can pass through together[22]. No peer-reviewed study of corker numbers exists. The only verified ratio is AusCycling's: at most one ride leader per 29 riders, with fewer riders per leader in rain, on hills or with novices (PRIMARY)[23]. The paper suggests at least one corker per conflicting approach at each held crossing, plus spares to leapfrog (JUDGEMENT).

What the app does

RouteMaker estimates corkers from the route[5]. It does not use a marshal ratio.

Which junctions need corkers

  • A crossing of an LTS 3, LTS 4 or Avoid road.
  • A left or right turn onto such a road. A right turn needs corkers too.
  • Quiet side streets (LTS 1 and 2) need none. Driveways and alleys are not counted.

How many at each junction

  • Approaches: 2 for a two-way road, 1 for a one-way road, 2 for a divided road, and 2 where it is not known.
  • No signal: one corker for each approach lane. Lanes not known count as 1.
  • At a signal: one corker per approach, whatever its lanes. The red light already holds cross traffic, and the corker covers the change to green.
  • Examples: a four-lane two-way road with no signal needs 4. The same road at a signal needs 2. A three-lane one-way street with no signal needs 3.

How many at once, and for the whole ride

A junction is held from when the head of the ride reaches it until the tail passes. A long group can hold several junctions at once. The most held at once, anywhere on the route, is the peak. Corkers leapfrog: when the tail passes, they ride up past the group to a junction ahead. The ride's headline is "about N corkers" = the peak × 2, rounded up. The factor of 2 is a proposal. Where only some junctions could be found, it says "at least about N".

Example: a downtown grid with a busy cross street every 1,200 ft [370 m], each needing 2 corkers, on 22 ft [6.7 m] of road. At 500 riders the group is about 1,560 ft [475 m] long and spans at most 2 busy junctions: 4 corkers at once, about 8 for the ride. At 2,000 riders it is about 1.18 mi [1.9 km] long and spans at most 6: 12 at once, about 24 for the ride.

Not built yet: a traffic circle or a diagonal avenue meeting the grid is still counted as one road's approaches, not by all its actual approaches.

Crossing clearance

The time to clear a crossing is the riders divided by the flow. The paper, at its central 90 riders a minute on one lane, gets about 2, 6 and 11 minutes for 200, 500 and 1,000 riders, and 8 to 19 minutes for 1,000 riders across its whole one-lane range (DERIVED). That is several signal cycles, which is the practical reason mass rides cork.

More: Crossing clearance

The app uses the same rule with its own flow. On one 11 ft lane that is about 2.0, 5.1 and 10.1 minutes for 200, 500 and 1,000 riders. On the riders chart, a dashed line shows what the ride size needs if no crossing is held longer than 5 minutes: riders ÷ 5 a minute. That is 100 for 500 riders, 20 for 100 and 400 for 2,000. The 5 minutes is a proposal[5].

The owner's video counts

The project owner counted riders in three DC Bike Party clips: two filmed from curb level and one from a mast about 10 ft [3 m] up[3]. The clips were filmed to count riders. The counts are the reliable part. The speeds and densities were a reuse of the same video, taken where the pack slowed for a turn, on different evenings and in different places. The owner puts every figure at about ±25%.

How the routing differs

Mass Ride sends the router a different set of settings[6]:

More: How the routing differs
  • Roads only. No trails or paths. Protected and painted bike lanes do not make a street cheaper: a group of hundreds uses the general roadway.
  • Never against traffic. Contraflow lanes are left out, so a route never rides a one-way street the wrong way.
  • The traffic slider is locked at 0, the direct end. A mile of LTS 3 or LTS 4 costs the same as a mile of quiet street. Roads rated Avoid still carry a large one-off charge to enter, as on every ride type.
  • Few turns. Each change of street costs 30 seconds, against the router's usual 5.
  • Hills are avoided strongly.
  • Pace for times: 6 mph [9.7 km/h], a parade pace.
  • Junctions rated Avoid are skipped where a way round adds no new LTS 4 or Avoid road and is at most 1 mi [1.6 km] or 25% longer, whichever is more.
  • RouteMaker's own search for calmer routes does not run. More on that search is on Routing.

Junction markers work differently too. A crossing of an LTS 3 road gets an orange marker. A crossing of an LTS 4 or Avoid road gets a red one. Signalized crossings within about a quarter mile [402 m] of each other are listed as one group, such as "6 signalized crossings".

Today the riders-per-minute figures describe a route. They do not yet choose it. Planned, and not built: a time charge for crossing busy roads, sized to the group; flagging and penalizing roads too narrow for the chosen ride size; a charge for changing between wide and narrow roads; and a capacity drop for sharp turns and poor pavement.

Usable width

Usable width is the road a corked group has in the direction it rides. The rules[4]:

More: Usable width
  • Own side only. Corkers hold the cross streets, not the oncoming traffic. So on a two-way street the ride has its own travel lanes and the painted bike lane on its side. It never counts the oncoming lanes or a shared center turn lane.
  • The narrower direction. The map does not know which way a ride will go, so a two-way segment shows its narrower direction.
  • One-way streets give every travel lane and every painted bike lane that runs with traffic. A contraflow lane is not counted.
  • Parked cars are never usable. Where OpenStreetMap shows parking but no width, a parallel parking lane takes 8 ft [2.4 m] a side[8].
  • Door zone. A painted bike lane beside parked cars loses 3.5 ft [1.07 m], the swing of an opened car door. A painted lane with no width given counts as 5 ft [1.5 m].
  • Protected bike lanes are never usable.
  • A lane with no width given counts as 11 ft [3.35 m]. A street with no lane count counts as a plain two-lane street: one lane each way.
  • In DC, the District's own Roadway Block data comes first[7]: lanes by direction, lane widths, bike lanes and parking. A way along several blocks takes its narrowest block. Bus lanes count.
  • Reversible lanes count as zero until each one is checked against current conditions. Connecticut Avenue NW's ended in 2020, though the data still lists them.
  • Very wide lanes. A DC block that records lanes of 16 ft [4.9 m] or more and no parking is read at 11 ft a lane. Such a "lane" is most likely shared by parked and moving cars.
  • Outside DC, and where the District's data has no plausible lane width (under 6 ft or over 20 ft), the width comes from OpenStreetMap.

The three route charts

A planned Mass Ride shows three charts, one under the other, in this order. They share one distance axis, so a mile is in the same place on all three. Moving along one chart moves the others and the marker on the map[5].

More: The three route charts
  1. Riders per minute. The figure along the route, with hills included, filled in the band colors, with dotted guides at 60, 120 and 200. The narrowest point is marked, and the route reports it twice: on the flat and with the hills. A dashed line shows what the ride size needs (see Crossing clearance). Stretches rated Avoid show "Avoid", not a number.
  2. Corker load. How many corkers are holding junctions at once, with the front of the ride at each point, and a tick at each junction that needs corkers. Stretches not checked for junctions are hatched and labeled "NOT CHECKED". The chart never shows zero where it does not know.
  3. Elevation. The elevation line[9], with grades of 5% to 8% and of 8% or more marked in amber.

Under the charts, a fold holds tables of the climbs, the bottlenecks and the junctions, with the corker load at each.

Hills

On the route's riders chart, the flat figure is lowered by the grade[4]:

More: Hills
  • Climbing past 1%, the group slows. The factor is 1 ÷ (1 + 12 × (grade − 1%)): 81% at 3%, 62.5% at 6%, 54% at 8%, and never under 30%. It builds up over the first 490 ft [150 m] of a climb, so a short ramp costs little and a long climb costs the full amount.
  • Descending past 4%, riders brake and leave more room. The factor is 1 ÷ (1 + 5 × (grade − 4%)): 83% at 8% down, and never under 60%. The pace is not raised.

Neither factor comes from a measurement. The climb factor agrees with a study of commuters up to about 6%, and is gentler above that[24]. No study of spacing on descents was found.

Hazard reports

Riders can mark stretches where rides have had problems. These reports are for Mass Ride only.

More: Hazard reports
  • A signed-in rider who is allowed to suggest changes picks a level and says what happened, in up to 500 characters.
  • Caution: riders have had problems here; take care. Drawn as a dotted rail.
  • Warning: riders have had serious problems here. Drawn as a dash-dot rail.
  • Nothing shows until a reviewer approves it. A reviewer can also delete it, or put it to the community as a poll.
  • Both levels stay routable. They warn; they do not steer the route. Only Avoid takes a stretch out.
  • A stretch already rated Avoid cannot be reported: the map shows only "AVOID" there.
  • Approved hazards are named in the legend, the road panel and the route's directions.
  • Privacy: the report is kept by road. The spot you clicked is checked to be within 98 ft [30 m] of the road, then dropped. Each rider can have at most 20 open reports, and one per road piece.

The literature check

After the app was built, each of its constants was checked against published bicycle flow studies[2]. Every published figure comes from everyday bicycle traffic: commuters on cycle tracks, queues at signals, or students on closed test tracks. Reported capacities vary about threefold between studies, so the literature can show whether a figure is plausible. It cannot set it precisely.

More: The literature check

What the check concluded:

  • The flat figure, 9.0 riders a minute per foot [29.5 per meter]: supported. Field counts run from about 5.6 to 22 per foot[19][16][15][17][18]. The model is in the lower half, and below every experiment[13][14]. Its closest match is the 10 ft [3 m] wide track at high density[12].
  • Density, one rider per 29 ft²: supported. Over the full width it is one rider per 42 ft², inside the range where flow holds steady and looser than a jam[12].
  • Share of width used, 0.7: partly supported, and probably low. Cycle tracks suggest about 0.75 to 0.9[16][15]. If video of wide streets shows 0.8 or more, their figures would rise by about 14%.
  • Pace, 4.3 mph: supported as the speed of a dense, moving flow, between about 1.8 and 4.3 mph[12][13]. Turning alone does not slow a rider that much: riders on their own took tight turns at about 6.6 mph [10.6 km/h][25]. The slowing at a turn is the group's own.
  • Hills: the climb factor is supported up to about 6%[24]. The descent factor is not checked: no study was found.
  • Width rules: an 11 ft lane is the top of current guidance (10 ft is typical in cities), so untagged streets may be read a little wide[10]. An 8 ft parking lane and a 3.5 ft door zone are supported[10][11].

No constant has been changed.
