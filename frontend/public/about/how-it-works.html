How RouteMaker works

RouteMaker plans bike routes between Washington, DC and Baltimore that keep you away from heavy traffic. It rates every road and path for how stressful its traffic feels on a bike, then looks for the route with the least stress for the kind of ride you chose.

In short

  • Every road gets a traffic-stress level, from LTS 1 (comfortable for everyone) to LTS 4 (busy, fast traffic), plus 4.5 and Avoid for the worst roads.
  • A level comes from the first of these that rates the road: community corrections, a local agency, a state agency, then RouteMaker's own formula.
  • Your ride type and the traffic slider decide how much extra distance a calmer road is worth. See Ride types.
  • Busy junctions cost extra too, so a quiet street that crosses a highway at a stop sign is not treated as quiet.
  • Real closures move a route. DC's planned-work permits never do.

The stress levels

RouteMaker uses Level of Traffic Stress (LTS), a four-level scale for bike riding developed by Mekuria, Furth and Nixon[1] and refined by Furth[2]. LTS 1 is meant to suit children; LTS 4 suits only the most traffic-tolerant riders. RouteMaker adds two levels of its own at the top, because the worst roads in this region are not all alike.

On the map each level also has its own dash pattern and line width, so the levels can be told apart without colour. The High contrast switch swaps to colours that do not rely on red and green.

Half steps

Some roads sit between two levels. A half step is drawn as its lower level, with a dashed line in the next level's colour from zoom 15. For routing, a half step costs the midpoint of its two levels.

LTS is an ordered scale, not a measurement: LTS 4 is not "twice" LTS 2[20]. So RouteMaker never averages levels. It turns each level into a cost per mile for each ride type instead (see From ratings to a route).

Which rating wins

Several sources can rate the same road. RouteMaker uses the first one that does, in this order:

  1. Community input. Corrections from riders and from RouteMaker's owner, for reasons such as fast drivers, poor sightlines or a dangerous merge. Each one is approved by hand; none is applied automatically.
  2. Local agency ratings. Montgomery County Planning's Bicycle Level of Traffic Stress[3] and DDOT's Bicycle Level of Traffic Stress in the District[4]. A county knows its roads better than a state does.
  3. State agency ratings. Maryland DOT State Highway Administration's statewide ratings[5], read from its adjusted score.
  4. The LTS formula. RouteMaker's own rating from OpenStreetMap[6] and street facts, described next. It rates every road the same way on both sides of every county and state line.

Separately from the levels, DC's own street records (posted speed, lanes, one-way streets, bike lanes and parking) are used instead of OpenStreetMap's tags in the District[4], because the District keeps them up to date.

Why the local agency wins

Inside Montgomery County both the county and the state rate many of the same roads. They mostly agree, but where they differ the county is usually the stricter one, and usually closer to how the road feels:

  • Rural roads upcounty (about 120 mi [190 km]), such as Peach Tree, Sugarland and Comus Roads: Maryland's formula scores an unstriped, low-traffic road LTS 1; the county rates many of them LTS 3 or 4. RouteMaker's own rural floor would not call them LTS 1 either.
  • Busy arterials (about 72 mi [116 km]), such as Darnestown Road, Georgia Avenue and Great Seneca Highway: Maryland says LTS 4; the county says 5, which RouteMaker reads as 4.5.
  • Where Maryland is stricter, as on Beach Drive (Maryland 4, the county 2), the county's rating still wins, and both show in the road panel.

Test cases

The roads where the two agencies disagree most are kept as test cases. Whenever the ratings or the matching change, these are the roads checked first: a wide gap is either a real difference in method, which the table explains, or a sign that two different roads were matched. Only 5.6 mi [9.0 km] of road differ by three levels (Maryland 2, the county 5), and most of that is matching, not method. Inside the county the county's level is used in every case.

What each place tells us

Every jurisdiction publishes different data, so the evidence behind a rating changes when you cross a line. The District publishes the most: its own stress ratings, street-by-street records of speed, lanes and bike lanes, traffic counts, pavement condition and its own closure feeds. Montgomery County publishes its own ratings. The rest of Maryland has the state's ratings on state roads only. Virginia publishes no stress ratings RouteMaker can use, so every Virginia road is rated by the formula from OpenStreetMap and traffic counts.

What this means for a ride:

  • The source can change mid-street. On Connecticut Avenue, DDOT's rating stops at the District line and the county's starts; the road panel names the source of each piece.
  • Where there is less agency data, ratings lean on OpenStreetMap tags and the cautious defaults above, so a rating there is likelier to be a little strict, and corrections from riders matter more.
  • Closures are uneven too: only Maryland and Virginia publish work-zone feeds; the District has police and emergency advisories and permits instead (see Closures).
  • DC's records win over OpenStreetMap for the same fact in the District, because the District updates them, many weekly.

The formula

Where no correction or agency rates a road, RouteMaker rates it from the published LTS tables[2], written fresh from the tables rather than copied from another tool. It works through these steps in order:

Speed

The posted limit wins. Where none is mapped, RouteMaker uses a cautious default: 20 mph [32 km/h] in the District, the District's legal default[9]; 25 to 35 mph [40 to 56 km/h] by road type in Maryland and Virginia towns; and 50 to 55 mph [80 to 89 km/h] on rural through roads. "Town" means inside a 2020 Census urban area[8]. An unpaved rural road is read at no more than 30 mph [48 km/h], and a narrow or winding rural road with no traffic count at 25 mph [40 km/h].

Bike lanes and lanes of traffic

A paved shoulder at least 4 ft [1.2 m] wide is read like a bike lane, and never worse than having none.

Traffic volume

On a road with one lane each way and no bike facility, traffic counts[10] move the level: 1,500 vehicles a day or fewer lowers it one level, and 8,000 or more raises it one level. Where one block's count is far above the rest of its street, usually traffic bunching at a junction, the street's typical count is used for the road and the higher count still counts at the junction.

Floors

Without a bike facility, trunk, primary and secondary roads are at least LTS 3, and tertiary roads at least LTS 2. A rough surface (dirt, grass, sand, cobbles) keeps a road out of LTS 1.

Special cases

  • Rural roads are at least LTS 2. LTS 1 is meant for children, and a rural road open to cars never qualifies, however quiet. Trails and roads closed to cars are not affected.
  • Quiet rural roads are not overrated. RouteMaker does not push a lightly used rural or gravel road to high stress on a default speed limit alone; it lowers a rating only for a clear reason, such as agency data or what riders report.
  • Rural expressways are LTS 4.5. A posted 50 mph [80 km/h] or faster expressway outside a town is very high stress, but often there is no way around it. The same road in a town is Avoid.
  • Motorways are closed to bikes unless signed open. One signed open to bikes is Avoid.
  • "Use the side path" roads (OpenStreetMap's bicycle=use_sidepath) are Avoid only where the side path is actually mapped beside them; otherwise they are rated like any other road.
  • Parking aisles and driveways open to bikes are treated as ordinary service roads, drawn on the map and usable for routing.
  • When it is unclear whether bikes are allowed, the way is treated as closed. Examples are military bases and secured government sites, ways signed no bicycles, and rated mountain-bike singletrack on ride types not meant for it. A closed way can be reopened by hand when there is evidence bikes may use it.
  • DC park paths. Paths in DC's circle parks (Dupont, Logan and others) are open to bikes, and sidewalks on National Park Service land in the Central Business District and on the Capitol grounds are exempt from the District's sidewalk ban. At memorials where you must walk, such as the Martin Luther King Jr. and Franklin Delano Roosevelt memorials, routes take you there and you walk the last bit.

Junctions

Crossing or turning onto a busy road is often the most stressful part of a ride, so junctions have a cost of their own, measured in calm miles: the extra distance of quiet street a junction is worth. Quiet streets meeting quiet streets cost almost nothing.

Junctions costing at least 800 ft [244 m] are marked orange in the route description, and those costing at least 3,100 ft [945 m] are marked red. Each warning describes the road, the movement and the control in words.

The control at a junction (a signal, a stop sign or none) comes from OpenStreetMap. From the version 0.10.0 map, the traffic signal lists of the District, Maryland's State Highway Administration, Virginia's DOT and Alexandria fill the gaps: a junction where the agency lists a signal and OpenStreetMap maps none is treated as signalled. Virginia's list is a snapshot from October 2023, so a signal built since then is not in it.

From ratings to a route

The routing engine is Valhalla[7], an open source router. RouteMaker builds its own copy of Valhalla's road graph each week with the stress levels written in, then tunes Valhalla's bike costing for each ride type. On top of that sits RouteMaker's own search, which asks Valhalla for alternatives, excludes busy stretches and bad crossings, and asks again, up to five rounds.

A route is chosen by adding up the cost of every stretch it rides, in calm miles. A mile of quiet street counts as 1. A busier stretch costs more, as if it were longer. On the Default ride type a mile of LTS 3 costs about 2.9 to 5.8 calm miles and a mile of LTS 4 about 13 to 16; faster and wider roads of the same level cost more. Each entry onto an Avoid road adds about 2.5 calm miles [4 km] more. Ride types has the figures for every ride type.

What a route pays for

  • Each stretch, by its stress level: the calm-mile costs above. Faster and wider roads of the same level cost more.
  • Each junction, by what you cross or turn across and what controls it: see Junctions.
  • Each entry onto an Avoid road: about 2.5 calm miles [4 km] more.
  • Riding the same road twice: a repeated stretch counts 1.5 times, but never at the price of more busy road.
  • Hills, by the Hills slider, and surface, by the ride type: rough DC pavement costs a little more, and each ride type has its own view of gravel and trails.
  • Closures: see Closures.

Choosing a ride type changes these numbers, not the ratings. It sets where the traffic slider starts, how much each level costs per mile, how fast you are assumed to ride, how hills and surfaces count, and for some types which ways are left out. Novice and Nervous reads every road and junction one level busier. Above 80 on the slider a second search looks for a calmer route and accepts a longer ride for it, up to a limit. Ride types has the detail.

What the comparison shows:

  • Default (slider 70) asks the routing engine once, with its own costs per level. It already keeps off busy roads: no LTS 4 at all, and most of the way on cycle tracks.
  • Trailmaxxing (slider 100) then runs the calm search: it asked for alternatives and left out the busiest stretches for two rounds, and took a different way down 17th Street, L Street and 11th Street. Its LTS 1 share rose from 73% to 85%. It rides slightly more LTS 3 (0.3 mi [0.5 km] against 0.26 mi), but much less LTS 2, so its stress exposure, the busy riding weighted by level, fell from 832 m to 654 m [2,730 ft to 2,150 ft].
  • Here the calmer route is also shorter. That is not the rule: the calm search accepts a longer route when it buys enough calm, up to a limit, and keeps Default's route when it finds nothing better. In two other trips tried the same day, the two ride types gave the same route.

Fast, at the other end of the slider, prices a mile of LTS 4 at only about 2.1 to 2.3 calm miles against Default's 13 to 16, so where a shorter way runs on busy roads, Fast takes it and Default does not.

Closures

Only Maryland and Virginia publish work-zone feeds in the national Work Zone Data Exchange format[17]: dated, mapped closures in force, with whether bikes can pass. The District publishes no such feed. What it has instead is a mix: police traffic advisories and AlertDC emergency closures[18], which name streets in words and are matched to the map; DDOT's TOPS permits[16], which say a street may be occupied some time in the coming month, not that it is closed now; and National Park Service alerts for the parks. So a closure in the District is usually known from an advisory or a reviewer, not from an official work-zone record, and planned roadwork in the District can be missing until someone reports it. Virginia's feed also needs an access key, so it is read only where one is set up.

  • State work zones from Maryland's and Virginia's feeds[17]: a road closed to bikes is left out; a closure that does not mention bikes costs about 10 minutes and gets a warning; lane and shoulder work cost a little.
  • Police and city alerts (MPD traffic advisories and AlertDC)[18] are matched to the streets they name and routed around.
  • Reviewed closures, entered or confirmed by a RouteMaker reviewer, are routed around and credited to the reviewer. Saved routes get an alert when one affects them.
  • Road closed in a ride marks the road ahead closed for that ride only. Where a route detours round a closure, the standard way is shown too, and you are followed on whichever you take.
  • DDOT TOPS permits[16] are permission to occupy a street some time in the coming month. They never move a route, in Go, Plan or anywhere else, and they raise no warnings. Plan has a DC permits layer, off by default, for anyone who wants to look.
  • National Park Service alerts[18] are park-wide notices, shown as a note on a route through the park, never as closures.

While you ride

  • Cues. The first cue comes about 27 seconds before a turn, the second about 10 seconds before, and "now" at the turn. Cues say only the turn and the street. You choose screen reader, RouteMaker's voice, both or neither.
  • Off route. You are off route when you are more than 100 ft [30 m] from the line (more when GPS is poor, up to 330 ft [100 m]) for three readings over 8 seconds. Readings less accurate than 160 ft [50 m] are not used.
  • Re-routing plans on from where you are, in the direction you are heading.
  • Stops. Tapping a food, sight, repair or water stop, or a lettered stop, opens a card with what is there, whether it is open now (where its mapped hours can be read), how far it is or the detour, and a Website link when one is mapped. A tap never changes the route: only Route here or Add as stop does. Stops come from OpenStreetMap, so check a repair stand works before you count on it.

What never changes a rating

  • Scenic roads. The Scenic layer (Montgomery County Rustic Roads, Maryland Scenic Byways, the East Coast Greenway, U.S. Bicycle Routes, covered bridges) is off by default and never changes a rating or a route.
  • Pavement. DC's Pavement Condition Index[19] makes a rough DC street cost a little more to ride, like a rougher surface; it never changes the street's stress level.
  • Hills. The Hills slider and the climb layer change effort, not stress.
  • Hazard reports on Mass Ride appear only on the Mass Ride map, after review.

Corrections and community input

Map data is not always right. A correction can set a road's level up or down, or set a minimum ("at least this level"). Riders who are signed in can suggest a level from the road panel. Every suggestion is reviewed, sometimes put to the community as a poll, and never applied automatically. Where a correction has a published note, a route that uses that road says why. Notes describe the road and its traffic, never a neighbourhood or its people.

Each weekly map rebuild produces a report of every rating that changed, by county, before the new map goes live. To report a wrong rating, open the road panel on the map and use Suggest a change. For DC streets, the ride screen can also send a report to DC 311.
